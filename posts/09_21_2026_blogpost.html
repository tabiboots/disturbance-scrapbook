How do we Disturb?

Reading Responses

“How would you know something doesn’t work if you don’t try? We tried many crazy things. Most were just crazy :)”
This attitude from the HK 19 Manual seems important to keep in mind as we try to disrupt the technological ecosystem that surrounds us. I think about virality and how unpredictable it is, yet how powerful it is for creating movements and cultural ideas. By trying any crazy idea that one comes up with, you give yourself a greater chance of success through sheer luck and exposure. Virality is a double-edged sword though. With the limelight comes scrutiny and attention. How can we cater within virality? Reach those we need to without becoming part of the zeitgeist itself. It’s also inspiring to see just how granular their roles became. Arrestee support, mental health support, online administrators, and more. Sometimes when an issue is as grand as the one Hong Kong was acting against, individuals cannot handle tackling the whole issue. Shrinking scope and delegating tasks across a trusted network is how we can grow our disruptions beyond ourselves.
Looking at another example of a guide to disturbance, we turn to beautifultrouble.org. I became interested in the Hashtag Hijack page.. The power of virality cannot be understated these days. It creates celebrities, movements, fanbases, and echo chambers. I recently read about the geo-political motivations that could have existed behind the proliferation of the “Dubai Chocolate” trend and it served as a reminder that virality is rarely accidental or innocuous. If this virality is controlled, then hijacking must be the way to regain power. While they highlight more playful examples of hashtag hijacking such as k-pop fans on twitter farming fancam views, I think there is a power to be explored within attacking trending hashtags with news or resources that would otherwise be difficult to spread to the masses.
Moving away from documents and towards examples, I was interested in the FlockHopper website, documenting the locations of over 130,000 flock cameras in America. I heard about a similar website earlier this week as news broke that Flock is taking legal action against the creators due to the exposure of their surveillance grid. I don’t need to highlight the absurd irony of this entire situation. The whole DeFlock movement has been fascinating to watch. There is something quite primal and cathartic about the more destructive aspects of this movement. When faced with overwhelming technological surveillance and power, we can still always bash in a computer with a rock and call it a day.
I was also struck by Mimi Onuoha's project "The Library of Missing Data Sets" I have often come back to the idea that algorithms are far from objective, an idea explored well in Cathy O'Neil's book Weapons of Math Destruction. I hadn't yet followed that idea to it's root and realized that even the existence of data is biased. The following quote from the projects repository captured the ideas I found interesting about this project quite well: "The word "missing" is inherently normative. It implies both a lack and an ought: something does not exist, but it should. That which should be somewhere is not in its expected place; an established system is disrupted by distinct absence. Just because some type of data doesn't exist doesn't mean it's missing, and the idea of missing data sets is inextricably tied to a more expansive climate of inevitable and routine data collection" There is a prevailing idea that we should have clean, complete datasets of almost everything that exists these days. The survailence and quantification of humanity that is tied to this idea can be overbearing, though. I am left considering what data sets might be missing, and which would be harmful to even collect?

Brainstorm

I think I want my project for this class to deal with the issue of humanizing online spaces or interactions. I want to achieve this through building a digital tool that can somehow help reshape trust or sovereignty online. I would like this project to continue to refine my data scraping and scripting skills, or maybe it can work on my web development and encryption skills. I am interested in learning more about the ways online spaces and dynamics have shifted due to the proliferation of hyper-realistic bots and what could rebuild trust in online interactions. Some of the half-baked ideas that are bouncing around in my head include:
  • A consolidator of online presence that includes some form of "human verification" such as uploading an image from your computer daily or sharing recent browsing history.

  • An extension that encrypts and decrypts social media posts through a key based system. A middleground between private and public accounts. How to still reach new people with similar ideas while avoiding botted interactions and knowing the others you interact with are intentional and real. I also think about projects like Infnite Storage Glitch that repurpose private, specializedcloud storage for more liberated and free uses.

  • Some kind of guide/resource to embody the other side of this dynamic. Through controlling these bots can we gain a greater understanding of how to operate around them?
One of the people I am hoping to chat with about this topic is Michael Mandiberg, a data artist in New York whose work happened to capture and catalog the internet prior to artificial content proliferation. His project Print Wikipedia happened to catalogue wikipedia prior to the spread of text generation, and his project Taking Stock did the same prior to artificial image generation. They are also an artist that is interested in the ways that large data systems shape society and self. I already am meeting with them for another reason this week and look forward to opening a discussion on this topic. I am curious about their Quantified Self Portrait project. I feel as though online activity is an already solitary act, and the way this project makes his computer activity public, and even part of his identity of self, is inspiring. I wonder if a social media esque way of sharing your browsing history is a way to make the internet feel more human, like pioneering a new path in a forest for others to follow you down.
I would also like to meet with people who have become interested in homelabbing or mesh networks in New York. I can reach out to my old professor Rory Solomon, who was active in the Mesh NYC space, and learn more about the ways that interacting with the internet changes when one feels in control of its content and flow.
Finally, I will be starting many conversations this week with those close to me that are passionate about similar issues. My friends from //PIXELMOUTH, my partner Lacey, Noah (my best friend from home). We all feel the effects of AI and the dead internet in our own ways and I am curious how it may have alienated them from certain aspects of the internet. What do we miss from the community we can find online? How can it be more trusted and how can it serve our lives in reality instead of isolate us further?
